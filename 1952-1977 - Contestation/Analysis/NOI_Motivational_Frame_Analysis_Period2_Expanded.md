# Motivational Framing in the Nation of Islam, 1952–1977: A Collective Action Frame Analysis Under Perspectival Critical Realism (Expanded Edition)

## Corpus Note

This is the **motivational** volume of a three-part frame analysis (Diagnostic / Prognostic / Motivational) of the Nation of Islam corpus, 1952–1977, drawn from the `1952-1977 - Contestation/` directory and selected for centrality to the Elijah Muhammad–Malcolm X contestation and the 1975 Warith Deen Muhammad succession, and for comparability with the Period 1 (1930–1951) motivational analysis. Sources are cited by short title; the full Works Cited (up to one hundred sources) appears at the end of this document and is shared, with minor emphasis differences, across all three volumes of this analysis.

This expanded edition draws on two bodies of previously uncited or underused material. First, Malcolm X's 1960–1963 public addresses on Elijah Muhammad's behalf — the Harlem Freedom Rally (1960), the Harvard Law School Forum (1961), the "Open Mind" roundtable (Oct. 1961), the Bayard Rustin debate (Oct. 1961), the Eleanor Fischer interview (1961), the Yale Law School Forum (Oct. 1962), the UC Berkeley speech (Oct. 1963), and the Columbia University speech (Nov. 1963) — supply a far fuller, pre-schism record of how M1 (Chosen Nation/Divine Urgency) and M4 (Racial Reversal/Cosmic Status Elevation) were actually delivered to live, often skeptical or hostile audiences, years before either frame's most famous single articulation ("God's Judgement of White America," Dec. 1963). Second, the fuller run of Warith Deen Muhammad's 1975 *Muhammad Speaks* series ("I Was Born for This Mission," "The Mark of the Beast," "Remake the World" Parts 1–3, "Black America's 'Mission' in the World," "Weapons," "Save Your Life," "A Growth Note: The Church," and the Feb. 26, 1975 Saviors Day Address, Pt. 2) considerably deepens the evidentiary base for M3 (Universal Brotherhood) and for M4's psychologized 1975 successor register. This revision also draws more heavily on the `Elijah Muhammad/Official_Publications` run of *Muhammad Speaks* (1960–1975), whose recurring "Do For Self," land-fund, and "We Must Have Some Land!" campaign material supplies M1 and M4 with a concrete, weekly-circulation evidentiary anchor beyond their more purely theological articulations in the books.

Motivational frames answer: *why should one act now, and on what basis is continued commitment demanded?* Six motivational frames meet the "removal test" (would the movement lose a significant reason to join, obey, sacrifice, or recruit without this frame?) and are presented here in more granular and more heavily evidenced form than the earlier edition of this report.

---

## EXECUTIVE SUMMARY: Ranked Motivational Frames

1. **M1 — Chosen Nation and Divine Urgency** — Elijah Muhammad's stable eschatological register (1957–1974): the audience is a specifically chosen people living at a divinely fixed and imminent turning point, a frame Malcolm X himself delivered fluently, in at least five distinct public settings, for more than three years before the 1964 schism.
2. **M2 — Loyalty and Hypocrisy: The Discourse of the Defector** — commitment enforced through a sharply binary distinction between loyal spokesmen and "hypocrites," applied with particular and lethal-register force to Malcolm X after March 1964.
3. **M4 — Racial Reversal and Cosmic Status Elevation** — the promise that the audience is not an oppressed minority but the divine "original" people of the earth, carried forward essentially unchanged from the Period 1 corpus, restated in Malcolm X's own pre-schism voice as late as December 1963, and independently reasserted, in a psychologized register, by Warith Deen Muhammad in 1975.
4. **M3 — Universal Brotherhood Under One God** — commitment grounded in a religiously constituted brotherhood that explicitly crosses the racial line the movement had previously treated as absolute, emerging independently in Malcolm X's 1964 Mecca experience and W. D. Muhammad's 1975 succession.
5. **M5 — This-Worldly Urgency: "Time Has Run Out"** — a this-worldly, politically contingent urgency distinct from M1's prophetic register, motivating immediate electoral and organizational action rather than patient awaiting of a fixed prophetic date.
6. **M6 — Sacrificial Witness: Courage Under Direct, Embodied Threat** — commitment motivated by the leader's own demonstrated willingness to accept personal risk and bodily harm, most fully documented in Malcolm X's public appearances in the days immediately following the firebombing of his home.

M1 and M2 jointly sustain Elijah Muhammad's frame package and are deployed with maximal, documented intensity at the exact moment (late 1964) that their underlying diagnostic claim is most directly and publicly contradicted by a former senior figure. M4 is the corpus's most durable inheritance from Period 1, recurring in at least four separate registers across the full twenty-two-year span, and the newly incorporated 1960–1963 material shows that Malcolm X was not merely a passive conduit for M1 and M4 but one of their most rhetorically capable public elaborators — a fact that sharpens, rather than softens, the significance of his later break with both frames. M3, M5, and M6 are specific to the 1964–1965 contestation and its 1975 aftermath, with no clear equivalent in Elijah Muhammad's stable middle-period corpus.

## DETAILED MOTIVATIONAL FRAME ANALYSIS

### M1 — Chosen Nation and Divine Urgency

**Definition.** Commitment and action are demanded on the basis that the audience is a specifically chosen people ("Lost-Found Nation of Islam," "tribe of Shabazz") living at a divinely fixed and imminent turning point ("the ending of the world... the world of the white man").

**Core Motivational Logic.** Time is not open-ended; it is prophetically bounded, and the movement's teacher possesses privileged, divinely sourced knowledge of its schedule. Action is owed not to strategic calculation but to obedience within a cosmic drama already underway — the direct continuation of the Period 1 corpus's apocalyptic-preparedness frame (Fard's "War of Armageddon," the *Supreme Wisdom Lessons*' Problem 15), now extended across four more decades of doctrinal restatement and, this expanded edition shows, actively re-elaborated in at least five separately dated public addresses by the movement's own national representative before he broke with it.

**Representative Quotations.**
- "There is much talk... about the ending of the world that we now live in (the world of the white man). The white race is losing their divine power to rule" (Elijah Muhammad, response to Malcolm X's departure, *Muhammad Speaks*, Sept. 11, 1964).
- Fard Muhammad to Elijah Muhammad, 1933 (predating but establishing the corpus's origin point): "Go right ahead with teaching righteousness to our own nation. Allah is with you... Your happiness is awaiting you on completion of your labor" — urgency framed as personal mission under direct divine commission from the movement's very founding document.
- "White America is doomed! God has declared that the Honorable Elijah Muhammad is your only means of escape. When you reject the Honorable Elijah Muhammad, when you refuse to hear his message or heed his warning you are closing your only door of escape" ("God's Judgement of White America," Malcolm X, Dec. 4, 1963) — delivered while Malcolm X was still the movement's most visible spokesman, this document shows M1 at its most fully elaborated, cast explicitly in the register of Noah, Lot, and Moses as prophetic "warners," with Elijah Muhammad positioned as their modern successor: "This little, meek, humble, inarticulate ex-slave is a modern Noah, a modern Lot, a modern Moses... a modern David."
- "According to the Scriptures... 'those who led others into captivity to go into captivity themselves'... this is justice! Other slave empires received justice, and now White America must receive justice" (same speech) — urgency is grounded not in contingent political calculation but in a claimed historical law (the fixed fate of prior "slave empires": Egypt, Babylon, Greece, Rome) applied deductively to the present.
- At the 1960 Harlem Freedom Rally, more than three years before the December 1963 speech, Malcolm X delivers a structurally identical urgency claim to a broad, religiously mixed civic audience: "The Western World, filled with evil and wickedness, is groping and stumbling blindly through spiritual darkness toward its inevitable doom... Allah has sent this last and greatest of all His Messengers, the Honorable Elijah Muhammad, to warn our people, and all the world, of the coming destruction" — evidence that M1's chosen-nation urgency was already fully formed in Malcolm X's public register at the very start of his decade as national spokesman, not something he grew into only by 1963.
- At the Harvard Law School Forum (March 24, 1961), Malcolm X gives the frame its most explicitly historical-eschatological cast, tying the "closing" of prophetic time to a specific, named 6,000-year reckoning: "God has come to close out the entire Old World, the Old World in which for the past 6,000 years practically the entire earth has been deceived, conquered, colonized, ruled, enslaved, oppressed and exploited by the Caucasian race... Every scripture, sacred and secular, testifies to a Great Reckoning, a Judgment, that is soon to take place" — the urgency here is explicitly framed as a matter of arithmetic (a bounded 6,000-year clock reaching its terminus), a more quantified variant of M1 than the 1963 speech's warner-typology register.
- The Eleanor Fischer interview (1961) shows the same urgency deployed in a more intimate, one-on-one broadcast register rather than a mass rally or university forum, evidence of M1's adaptability across venue: Malcolm X describes Elijah Muhammad's teaching as arriving "at a time when our people need it most," implicitly presenting the present historical moment, not any other, as providentially suited to reception of the message.
- The UC Berkeley speech (Oct. 11, 1963) restates the urgency claim just weeks before "God's Judgement of White America," with an explicit call to immediate, individual decision rather than passive spectatorship: listeners are pressed to recognize that "time" itself, not merely political circumstance, is the object running out — continuous with, and a direct rhetorical precursor to, the December address's fuller elaboration.

**Connection to Diagnostic Frames.** Directly derived from D1 (Diagnostic volume): only a fixed, cosmologically ordered antagonist permits a fixed, prophetically scheduled resolution.

**Connection to Prognostic Frames.** Bound to P1 (Divine Separation): "this is the time of our separation and the judgment of this world" collapses the distinction between strategic timing and prophetic timing entirely.

**Empirical Level.** Present from the corpus's earliest document (the 1933 Fard letter) through its latest Elijah Muhammad-authored document (1974) — a forty-one-year span, the longest continuity of any frame identified in either period of this analysis — and independently attested in at least five separately dated Malcolm X addresses between 1960 and 1963, a considerably larger empirical base for the frame's pre-schism currency than the original report's single-speech citation implied.

**Actual Level.** Functions to insulate the movement's authority structure from ordinary political contingency: setbacks, defections, and legal difficulties (documented independently in the FBI files) are represented as expected features of prophetic time rather than as evidence against leadership. Malcolm X's own repeated, venue-adapted articulations of M1 — the civic register at Harlem in 1960, the historical-arithmetic register at Harvard in 1961, the intimate broadcast register with Fischer, the university-forum register at Berkeley in 1963 — are themselves direct evidence of how completely and how skillfully this frame could be internalized, varied, and redeployed by a figure who would, within months of the last of these addresses, abandon it entirely — underscoring that M1's power in this corpus operated independently of any individual speaker's later trajectory, and that its eventual repudiation by Malcolm X represented the loss of one of its most capable exponents, not merely a peripheral figure's dissent.

**Real Level (Retroduction).** *Retroductive inference:* the frame's extraordinary temporal stability, unmodified across a documented external shock as severe as Malcolm X's public departure and later assassination, suggests the frame's function was, in part, to render such shocks doctrinally unsurprising and thus non-threatening to continued membership commitment. The frame's demonstrated adaptability across at least four distinct public registers, in the hands of a single skilled spokesman, further suggests M1 functioned less as a rigid script than as a generative interpretive resource capable of being retailored to markedly different audiences (mass rally, elite law-school forum, broadcast interview, university lecture hall) while preserving its core temporal claim — a versatility that may help explain both the frame's durability under Elijah Muhammad and Malcolm X's later capacity to construct, with comparable rhetorical skill, the very different urgency register of M5. This is inferred from the frame's behavior under strain and across venues, not from any statement in the corpus explicitly describing this as the frame's purpose.

**Relative Importance:** **High** throughout Elijah Muhammad's leadership; **directly and explicitly de-emphasized** (not eliminated) under W. D. Muhammad in 1975, who redirects motivational language toward institution-building urgency (see Prognostic Frame P4).

**Evidence Across Corpus:** MX_1960_Speech_HarlemFreedomRally; MX_1961_03_24_Speech_HarvardLawForum; MX_1961_Interview_EleanorFischer; MX_1963_10_11_Speech_UCBerkeley; MX_1963_12_04_Speech_GodsJudgementOfWhiteAmerica (as movement spokesman, pre-schism); EM_1964_09_11_Article_ResponseToMXLeaving; TFOA (1973); OSHA (1974).

### M2 — Loyalty and Hypocrisy: The Discourse of the Defector

**Definition.** Commitment is demanded and enforced through a sharply binary discourse distinguishing loyal, legitimate spokesmen from "hypocrites" and "defectors" — a category applied with particular force and specificity to Malcolm X after March 1964.

**Core Motivational Logic.** The frame does not merely describe disagreement; it delegitimizes the very act of public dissent by an insider as a moral, not merely strategic, betrayal, converting policy or theological disagreement into a question of personal loyalty and even mortal desert. The frame's force is sharpened, this expanded edition shows, by the fact that the movement's own pre-schism record establishes Malcolm X's total prior fluency in the very doctrines he came to be accused of betraying, foreclosing any reading of his departure as ignorance or confusion.

**Representative Quotations.**
- Louis X (later Louis Farrakhan), December 4, 1964: describing Malcolm X as "defector and hypocrite Malcolm" whose "shameful attempts... to slander and destroy the great Islamic leader" had "caused concern not only among the ranks of Muslims, but among many fair-minded non-Muslims."
- Same article: "Such a man as Malcolm is made... Worthy of death, and would have met with death if it had not been for Muhammad's confidence in Allah for victory over the enemies."
- Elijah Muhammad's own September 1964 restatement of doctrine, published in direct response to Malcolm X's departure, does not name him but reasserts, at length and without concession, the very doctrines Malcolm X had just publicly begun to depart from — itself a motivational act of reaffirmed loyalty-demand through doctrinal restatement rather than direct personal address.
- Malcolm X's own account of the FBI's February 1964 visit to warn him of threats against his life, read alongside the December 1964 "worthy of death" language, situates the loyalty-and-betrayal discourse within a documented, lived climate of genuine physical danger rather than as purely rhetorical excess.
- The severity of the "hypocrite" charge is thrown into sharper relief by Malcolm X's own, pre-schism use of the identical category against others: at the Harvard Law School Forum (1961) and elsewhere in the 1960–1963 addresses, Malcolm X routinely distinguishes the Nation of Islam's own "sincere" Black leadership from "so-called Negro leaders" whom he characterizes as compromised intermediaries serving white interests — establishing that the loyalty/hypocrisy binary was not invented in 1964 to deal with Malcolm X specifically but was an established rhetorical structure he himself had deployed against others for years, which he then had turned against him.

**Connection to Diagnostic Frames.** The "hypocrite" label functions as a diagnostic category in its own right (Diagnostic Frame D4) — it diagnoses the defector's stated reasons for leaving as pretextual, thereby foreclosing engagement with the substance of Malcolm X's post-departure diagnostic revisions.

**Connection to Prognostic Frames.** Functions to discredit, by association with the discredited person, any prognosis (P2, P3, P5, P6) that Malcolm X subsequently proposed, independent of that prognosis's substantive content.

**Empirical Level.** Directly documented in Louis X's December 1964 article — including the explicit "worthy of death" language, published in *Muhammad Speaks* roughly ten weeks before Malcolm X's assassination on February 21, 1965 — and structurally anticipated in Malcolm X's own pre-1964 rhetorical practice of distinguishing loyal from compromised Black spokesmen.

**Actual Level.** This is a point requiring particular evidentiary caution. The corpus contains a documented published statement describing Malcolm X as "worthy of death" prior to his assassination. **This is direct textual evidence of the existence and publication of such language.** It is **not**, on the strength of this corpus alone, evidence of a causal link between this specific publication and the assassination itself, which involved separate individuals, a criminal investigation, and a historical record outside the scope of the sampled documents. This report records the frame's documented content and its documented timing; it does not draw a causal inference the corpus cannot support.

**Real Level (Retroduction).** *Retroductive inference, clearly bounded:* the intensity and personalization of this loyalty-enforcement language in late 1964 is consistent with a generative mechanism in which the movement's authority structure, built substantially on the personalized charismatic and prophetic frames above (M1), had few available registers for absorbing public, doctrinally grounded dissent from a former senior minister other than total moral delegitimization of the dissenter — a mechanism made more, not less, plausible by the newly incorporated evidence that Malcolm X himself had helped construct and repeatedly deploy this very binary against others before it was turned against him. This is offered strictly as one plausible mechanism explaining the frame's severity, not as an account of intent or of any specific actor's motives regarding the events of February 1965.

**Relative Importance:** **High** specifically at the moment of schism (1964); the frame recedes, without being formally repudiated, under the reintegrative approach of 1975 ("we will not dig it up").

**Evidence Across Corpus:** LF_1964_12_04_Article_BiggestHypocrite; EM_1964_09_11_Article_ResponseToMXLeaving; MX_1964_02_04_VisitFromFBI; MX_1961_03_24_Speech_HarvardLawForum.

### M3 — Universal Brotherhood Under One God

**Definition.** Commitment is grounded not in racial chosen-ness (M1, M4) but in the experience of a religiously constituted brotherhood that explicitly crosses the racial line the movement had previously treated as absolute.

**Core Motivational Logic.** Direct, embodied experience is offered as motivationally superior to received doctrine — experience overturns prior teaching. "We were truly all the same (brothers) — because their belief in one God had removed the 'white' from their minds" (Malcolm X, Mecca Letter, April 20, 1964).

**Representative Quotations.**
- Malcolm X's fuller account of the same experience, delivered later that year to an American audience: "When I got over there and went to Mecca and saw these people who were blond and blue-eyed and pale-skinned and all those things, I said, 'Well!' But I watched them closely. And I noticed that though they were white, and they would call themselves white, there was a difference between them and the white one over here" (speech, 1964) — the motivational force of the frame is presented as arising from close, skeptical, first-hand observation rather than credulous acceptance, which is itself part of the frame's rhetorical claim to authority: "I am very pleased to see so many who have come out to always see for yourself, where you can hear for yourself, and then think for yourself."
- Malcolm X's explicit account of his own prior belief and its overturning: "Elijah Muhammad had taught us that the white man could not enter into Mecca in Arabia... he's white and inherently evil, it's impossible to change him... So when I got over there... I watched them closely" — direct first-person evidence of the diagnostic reversal (D1's abandonment) generating this motivational frame, not the reverse.
- W. D. Muhammad's first official interview (March 21, 1975) supplies the fullest, most deliberate installment of M3 in the 1975 succession material, framed explicitly as a correction rather than a rupture: he describes his father's teaching as necessary "medicine" for a specific historical moment of Black demoralization, then states that the moment for that medicine has passed and that the community must now "grow up" into a wider, non-racially-bounded fellowship — motivational force is here drawn from the language of maturation ("growing up") rather than from a single dramatic conversion experience of the kind Malcolm X describes.
- W. D. Muhammad's "I Was Born for This Mission" (March 21, 1975) extends the same logic into a personal vocational register: he presents his own succession itself, and his authority to redirect the movement toward inclusion, as a providentially assigned "mission" continuous with, rather than a betrayal of, his father's larger purpose — a rhetorical strategy that lets M3 do universalizing work while still drawing motivational force from M1's inherited chosen-mission register.
- "if the white man respects us, we will respect him" (W. D. Muhammad, as reported in *Time*, June 1975), consistent with his own recorded statements on ending the stress on "the white man's past atrocities" — brotherhood here is explicitly conditional and reciprocal rather than unconditional, a notably different motivational structure from Malcolm X's Mecca-derived account of an experience that simply and immediately "removed the 'white' from their minds."

**Connection to Diagnostic Frames.** Directly incompatible with D1 and requires either its abandonment (Malcolm X, 1964) or its careful historical bounding ("the past is the past... we will not dig it up," W. D. Muhammad, 1975) rather than its reassertion.

**Connection to Prognostic Frames.** Underwrites P3 (Pan-African internationalization, which explicitly includes white sympathizers and international Muslim solidarity) and P4 (domestic reintegration).

**Empirical Level.** Directly stated in the Mecca Letter (1964) and echoed, in a domestically oriented and more explicitly conditional register, in W. D. Muhammad's 1975 statements — an eleven-year gap between two independent, non-identical articulations of a structurally similar motivational shift by two men who both had a direct personal and institutional relationship to Elijah Muhammad.

**Actual Level.** Functions to reconstitute the basis of belonging from ascribed racial status to shared religious experience or civic reconciliation — a change with direct organizational consequences in both cases (the OAAU's stated inclusion of "brothers and sisters" defined by African descent rather than religious doctrine; W. D. Muhammad's stated willingness to admit white members).

**Real Level (Retroduction).** *Retroductive inference:* the appearance of structurally similar universalizing motivational shifts in two men whose personal relationship (mentor's son and estranged senior minister) was otherwise antagonistic, occurring roughly a decade apart under very different immediate circumstances (pilgrimage experience versus succession to leadership), is consistent with — but does not prove — a shared underlying pressure toward doctrinal universalization once movement figures gained direct, sustained contact with the international, non-racialized Muslim world. The corpus contains no direct statement from either man citing the other's prior articulation; this parallel is treated as suggestive, not demonstrated.

**Relative Importance:** **High** at both points of emergence (1964; 1975); **absent** from Elijah Muhammad's own corpus in the sampled period.

**Evidence Across Corpus:** MX_1964_04_20_Letter_FromMecca; MX_1964_08_21_Speech_AfricanSummitConference; IWDM_1975_03_21_Interview_FirstInterview; IWDM_1975_03_21_Article_IWasBornForThisMission; LF_1975_06_25_Article_TimeMagazine.

### M4 — Racial Reversal and Cosmic Status Elevation

**Definition.** Members are taught they are not an oppressed minority but the "original," divine people of the earth, while whites are a manufactured, temporary, and inferior "grafted" race whose rule is nearing its scientifically predicted expiration — the corpus's most direct continuation of a named Period 1 frame (Period 1 Motivational Frame 2), now documented across three additional decades, actively re-elaborated in Malcolm X's own pre-schism voice, and in its final 1975 iteration transposed from a biological to a psychological register.

**Core Motivational Logic.** Reversal of stigma into supremacy. Rather than seeking equality within the existing racial order, the frame offers total inversion: Black members become "the Gods," while the antagonist's rule is cosmologically doomed and, in due course, ends.

**Representative Quotations.**
- "White America's worst crime[:] her hypocrisy and her deceit... the Honorable Elijah Muhammad's mission... is to remind America that God has not forgotten America's crimes against his long-lost people" (Malcolm X, "God's Judgement of White America," 1963) — the reversal here operates at the level of moral and historical judgment: the formerly stigmatized become the judges, the formerly powerful become the judged.
- At the Harlem Freedom Rally (1960), Malcolm X delivers an early, more populist-register installment of the same reversal, directed at instilling collective pride rather than issuing judgment: he tells the mixed civic audience that they are heirs to "a great civilization" and a "great history" deliberately hidden from them, so that the reversal is framed first as *restoration of stolen knowledge* rather than *cosmic promotion* — a subtly different motivational emphasis (recovery of what is rightfully already theirs) from the more explicitly supernatural "original people of the earth" register found in the doctrinal books.
- At the Columbia University speech (Nov. 20, 1963), delivered two weeks before "God's Judgement of White America," Malcolm X restates the reversal in explicitly comparative-civilizational terms before an academic audience, contrasting the accomplishments attributed to ancient Black civilizations with the "so-called" backwardness taught in American schoolbooks — the reversal frame adapted, once again, to a specific, skeptical, credential-conscious audience.
- "This world, the world of grafted Caucasian mentality, has grown old... It has grown so hopeless today" and "Justice is come and everybody is being put on an equal footing... You not only have justice, but you have a Divine blessing too" (W. D. Muhammad, "The Death of White Supremacy," July 18, 1975) — the identical status-reversal promise as the founding cosmology, restated with the racial category ("Caucasian") retained but reattached to "mentality" rather than heredity (see Diagnostic Frame D6), demonstrating that M4's motivational force survived the diagnostic revision essentially intact even as its underlying metaphysics changed.
- "We know where the power is. The power is with the righteous and we are the righteous" (same document) — a direct restatement, in 1975, of the Period 1 corpus's core motivational promise ("the Gods of the universe") with no loss of intensity despite the intervening forty years and the intervening diagnostic revision.
- W. D. Muhammad's "Who Is The Original Man?" (Aug. 22, 1975) supplies the fullest 1975 restatement of the "original man" promise itself, retaining the term but redirecting its emotional payoff away from racial supremacy and toward a more universal claim about human moral origin and responsibility — evidence that even the frame's core vocabulary ("original man") could be carried forward by the son while its supremacist emotional charge was deliberately, and explicitly, dialed down.
- The frame's earliest and most didactic sampled installment in *Muhammad Speaks* pairs the reversal directly with a call to abandon internalized inferiority: "Throw Out Those White Dolls! Oh Black Man, Wake Up!... A part of the reason so-called American Negroes have such a low sense of race pride can be found in the simple but viciously subtle propaganda of white..." (Vol. 1, No. 4, Feb. 1962) — status elevation here is presented not as an abstract cosmological fact to be believed but as an active, remedial break from a specific, named, and demonstrably present psychological injury (doll preference among Black children, an allusion to the Clark doll studies then current in desegregation-era social science), giving M4 a this-worldly, evidentiary anchor absent from its more purely theological articulations elsewhere in the corpus.
- A companion editorial cartoon in the same run instructs the reader directly: "BE NOT AFRAID OF YOUR OWN... [white men may have different religious beliefs, different politics, different customs, they can resolve]" (Vol. 1, No. 2, Dec. 1961) — a rare instance of M4's reversal logic turned inward, addressed not to the reversal of white supremacy but to the members' own residual fear of intra-communal difference, suggesting the frame also did local, in-group cohesion work beyond its more famous external, racial-cosmological register.
- The "We Must Have Freedom, Justice, and Equality" front-page campaign in *Muhammad Speaks*'s inaugural issue (Vol. 1, No. 1, May 1960) fuses M4's status claim directly to a concrete institutional demand, presenting the paper's very first readers with reversal-as-entitlement rather than reversal-as-abstraction: the "original" people's cosmic status is invoked as the ground of an immediate, practical claim on land and resources, not held apart from it as a purely devotional consolation.

**Connection to Diagnostic Frames.** Directly derived from D1 in Elijah Muhammad's corpus and from its 1975 psychologized successor, D6, in W. D. Muhammad's corpus — evidence that M4 is more durable across doctrinal revision than the specific diagnostic frame that originally generated it.

**Connection to Prognostic Frames.** Underwrites P1 (separation, in Elijah Muhammad's corpus) and, in its 1975 form, P4 (reintegration) — a striking instance of the same motivational frame licensing opposite prognoses at different moments, because the promised status elevation is compatible with either "exit from" or "renewed standing within" a shared world, depending on how the antagonist is currently diagnosed.

**Empirical Level.** Explicit across the full sampled span: 1960 (Malcolm X, Harlem Freedom Rally), 1963 (Malcolm X, Columbia University and "God's Judgement of White America," as movement spokesman), 1965 (Elijah Muhammad's post-schism restatement), 1975 (W. D. Muhammad's doctrinal series) — direct evidence of continuity independent of leadership change, and of the frame's presence at the very start, not only the climax, of Malcolm X's public career as spokesman.

**Actual Level.** Manifests in the corpus as a persistent redescription of the movement's entire mission as a project of *judgment upon*, rather than *appeal to*, the dominant society — a stance documented consistently regardless of which specific prognosis (separation or reintegration) it is paired with at a given moment, and regardless of whether the immediate rhetorical register is populist civic pride (Harlem, 1960), academic comparative civilization (Columbia, 1963), prophetic judgment (December 1963), or psychologized moral universalism (1975).

**Real Level (Retroduction).** *Retroductive inference:* the plausible generative mechanism, consistent with the Period 1 analysis's own retroductive finding, is status inversion as psychological compensation for structural domination that ordinary political channels could not redress — a mechanism this corpus suggests remained operative across radically different institutional conditions (a small, marginal 1930s sect; a large, self-sufficient mid-1960s organization with substantial property holdings; a legitimacy-seeking 1975 successor organization), which is itself evidence of the mechanism's independence from any one organizational form. The frame's demonstrated capacity to be recast in at least four distinct rhetorical registers by two different men, a decade apart, without losing its underlying reversal structure, further suggests the *structure* of the promise (stigmatized-become-elevated) was more essential to its motivational function than any single doctrinal vehicle (biological Yacub etiology, "grafted mentality," or civilizational-historical recovery) for delivering it.

**Relative Importance:** **High** throughout the full 1952–1977 period and, read alongside the Period 1 analysis, across the movement's entire documented history to that point.

**Evidence Across Corpus:** MX_1960_Speech_HarlemFreedomRally; MX_1963_11_20_Speech_ColumbiaUniversity; MX_1963_12_04_Speech_GodsJudgementOfWhiteAmerica; EM_1964_09_11_Article_ResponseToMXLeaving; MTTBIA (1965); IWDM_1975_07_18_MS_Article_TheDeathOfWhiteSuprememacy; IWDM_1975_08_22_MS_Article_WhoIsTheOriginalMan; MS Vol. 1, No. 1 (May 1960), "We Must Have Freedom, Justice, and Equality"; MS Vol. 1, No. 2 (Dec. 1961), "Be Not Afraid of Your Own" cartoon; MS Vol. 1, No. 4 (Feb. 1962), "Throw Out Those White Dolls! Oh Black Man, Wake Up!"

### M5 — This-Worldly Urgency: "Time Has Run Out"

**Definition.** A this-worldly, politically contingent urgency, distinct from M1's prophetic register, motivating immediate electoral, legal, and organizational action on the ground that ordinary political time — not cosmic time — is running out.

**Core Motivational Logic.** Unlike M1, where urgency is fixed by an unfalsifiable prophetic schedule, M5's urgency is conditional and empirically arguable: it is grounded in specific, checkable political facts (upcoming elections, specific legislative sessions, specific incidents of violence) and is therefore, in principle, responsive to changed political circumstances in a way M1 by design is not.

**Representative Quotations.**
- "If we don't do something real soon, I think you'll have to agree that we're going to be forced either to use the ballot or the bullet. It's one or the other in 1964. It isn't that time is running out — time has run out!" (Malcolm X, "The Ballot or the Bullet," April 3, 1964).
- "1964 threatens to be the most explosive year America has ever witnessed... The year when all of the white politicians will be back in the so-called Negro community jiving you and me for some votes" — urgency here is keyed to the electoral calendar itself, a form of time-pressure with no equivalent in M1.
- "So, where do we go from here? First, we need some friends. We need some new allies. The entire civil-rights struggle needs a new interpretation" (same speech) — the urgency is explicitly paired with a demand for strategic adaptation, in contrast to M1's demand for patient doctrinal fidelity.
- The interview with A. B. Spellman (March 19, 1964), conducted one week into Malcolm X's independence, shows the frame still under construction: urgency in this document is expressed mainly in organizational terms — the need to build Muslim Mosque, Inc.'s political apparatus quickly — rather than in the fully secularized, calendar-specific register the April speeches would achieve within two weeks, evidence that M5, like the related diagnostic and prognostic frames it underwrites, emerged through a rapid, real-time process rather than arriving fully formed at the moment of the organizational break.

**Connection to Diagnostic Frames.** Directly derived from D2 (institutional-political exclusion) and D5 (government conspiracy): urgency is generated by the closing of a specific, describable political window (the 1964 elections; the ongoing filibuster of civil rights legislation), not by an unfalsifiable prophetic clock.

**Connection to Prognostic Frames.** Bound tightly to P2 (electoral mobilization) and P5 (armed self-defense as the branching alternative if the electoral channel proves closed) — the frame's entire rhetorical structure ("the ballot or the bullet") is built as a conditional branch keyed to whether P2's mechanism is permitted to function.

**Empirical Level.** Stated directly, repeatedly, and with specific institutional detail in the April 1964 speeches, with an earlier, less fully secularized version visible in the March 19 Spellman interview.

**Actual Level.** The frame does the work of making the year 1964 itself — rather than an unspecified prophetic future — the operative unit of urgency, a marked departure from M1's structure and one directly tied to Malcolm X's newly independent political register following his March 1964 departure.

**Real Level (Retroduction).** *Retroductive inference:* the substitution of a bounded, checkable political deadline for M1's unfalsifiable prophetic one is consistent with a generative mechanism in which loss of access to the Nation of Islam's prophetic authority structure required Malcolm X to ground urgency in a register his new, religiously mixed audience ("Baptist, or a Methodist, or a Muslim, or a nationalist") could independently verify and share, rather than one requiring prior acceptance of NOI theology. The short interval between the Spellman interview's still organizationally focused urgency and the fully calendar-bound urgency of the April speeches is consistent with, though it does not itself prove, a rapid process of public frame-testing under the pressure of establishing an independent following within weeks of the break. The corpus does not contain a document in which Malcolm X explicitly reasons through this substitution; it is inferred from the contrast between his 1963, March 1964, and April 1964 rhetorical registers.

**Relative Importance:** **High** for the specific eighteen-month window (1963–1965) of the sharpest documented frame contestation in the corpus; **absent** as a structural feature of Elijah Muhammad's or W. D. Muhammad's corpus, both of which retain some form of urgency keyed to a fixed schedule (prophetic in Elijah Muhammad's case, institutional/financial in W. D. Muhammad's) rather than to an electoral calendar.

**Evidence Across Corpus:** MX_1964_03_19_Interview_ABSpellman; MX_1964_04_03_Speech_BallotOrBullet; MX_1964_04_12_Speech_BallotOrBullet (Cleveland variant); MX_1964_03_12_DeclarationOfIndependence.

### M6 — Sacrificial Witness: Courage Under Direct, Embodied Threat

**Definition.** Commitment is motivated not by doctrine or promised reward but by the leader's own demonstrated, personally witnessed willingness to accept bodily risk — a frame documented with unusual immediacy in Malcolm X's public appearances in the days following the February 1965 firebombing of his home, and with no direct equivalent, in this specific embodied register, elsewhere in the sampled corpus.

**Core Motivational Logic.** The leader's continued public presence, visibly marked by recent physical danger, is itself offered as the argument for continued commitment — proof of sincerity that supersedes any propositional claim requiring separate justification. The frame does not primarily ask the audience to believe a doctrine; it asks them to recognize demonstrated courage and to measure their own commitment against it.

**Representative Quotations.**
- "I was in a house last night that was bombed, my own. It didn't destroy all my clothes, not all, but you know what happens when fire dashes through — they get smoky. The only thing I could get my hands on before leaving was what I have on now... I don't normally come out in front of people without a shirt and a tie" (Malcolm X, Ford Auditorium, Feb. 14, 1965) — the apology for his own appearance is itself the motivational device, converting a personal vulnerability into evidence of priority: appearing at all, under these conditions, outweighs appearing correctly dressed.
- "It isn't something that made me lose confidence in what I am doing, because my wife understands and I have children from this size on down, and even in their young age they understand. I think they would rather have a father or brother or whatever the situation may be who will take a stand in the face of any kind of reaction from narrow-minded people rather than to compromise and later on have to grow up in shame and in disgrace" (same speech) — courage is explicitly framed as a legacy owed to one's children, generalizing the motivational appeal beyond the immediate audience to an implied intergenerational audience.
- "In trying to get my family out of the house, none of us stopped for any clothes at that point — twenty-degree cold... a doctor came today... shot something in my arm that naturally put me to sleep... there's nothing like sleeping through a whole lot of excitement" — the wry, understated tone is itself part of the motivational device: danger is acknowledged fully but processed without evident fear, modeling the demeanor the frame implicitly asks the audience to adopt toward their own risk.
- The frame's clearest documented precursor is Malcolm X's own November 1963 "Message to the Grassroots," delivered while he remained the Nation of Islam's spokesman: "You bleed when the white man says bleed... How can you justify being nonviolent in Mississippi and Alabama, when your churches are being bombed... at the same time you're going to get violent with Hitler" — here the courage-appeal is still comparative and hypothetical (contrasting the audience's professed nonviolence with their willingness to fight abroad), rather than the fully embodied, first-person register of the 1965 Ford Auditorium speech, in which the danger being described is the speaker's own, immediate, and ongoing.

**Connection to Diagnostic Frames.** Directly derived from D5 (state surveillance and government conspiracy) and, by direct implication though the corpus does not draw the causal line explicitly, from D4 (the hypocrite/defector discourse's documented "worthy of death" language, published ten weeks before the firebombing) — M6 is the motivational frame generated by living, in the first person, the exact conditions D4 and D5 together diagnose.

**Connection to Prognostic Frames.** Reinforces P5 (armed self-defense) retroactively and concretely: a listener in February 1965 encountering Malcolm X's account of the firebombing has direct, immediate, personal evidence for the March 1964 prognosis's premise that "the government seems unable or unwilling to protect" Black Americans from organized violence.

**Empirical Level.** Directly and vividly documented in Malcolm X's own first-person account, delivered the evening after the event, with specific sensory detail (the smoke smell in his clothing, the twenty-degree cold, the sedative administered by the attending doctor), and structurally anticipated fifteen months earlier in the comparative courage-appeal of "Message to the Grassroots."

**Actual Level.** The frame's actual-level referent — Malcolm X's assassination one week later, on February 21, 1965 — lies just beyond the corpus's own documentary window for this specific speech but is essential context for interpreting its stakes; this report records the documented speech and its documented date without treating the subsequent assassination as itself part of the frame's textual content.

**Real Level (Retroduction).** *Retroductive inference, explicitly bounded:* the frame's motivational power plausibly derives from the audience's direct, simultaneous knowledge that the danger being described was current and unresolved, not historical — a structurally different motivational mechanism from M1's or M4's appeal to a promised future vindication, and one that appears to have developed out of the more hypothetical courage-appeal of "Message to the Grassroots" as Malcolm X's own circumstances shifted from rhetorical comparison to lived, embodied threat. The corpus does not contain audience-response documents (letters, membership records) that would allow this inference to be tested against evidence of the frame's actual reception; it is offered as a plausible reading of the speech's own rhetorical construction and its relationship to the earlier, less embodied register.

**Relative Importance:** **High** for the specific, brief final week of Malcolm X's public life; without clear precedent or successor in the rest of the sampled corpus in its fully embodied form, though structurally continuous with the "afraid to bleed" courage-appeal of the November 1963 "Message to the Grassroots."

**Evidence Across Corpus:** MX_1965_02_14_Speech_AfterFirebombing_FordAuditorium; MX_1963_11_10_Speech_MessageToTheGrassroots (precursor register); MX_1964_02_04_VisitFromFBI (documented advance warning of threat).

## COMPARATIVE NOTE: Continuity and Change from Period 1

M1 (Chosen Nation/Divine Urgency) and M4 (Racial Reversal/Cosmic Status Elevation) are the corpus's most direct continuations of Period 1 motivational frames (Period 1 Frames 4 and 2, respectively), and the Period 1 analysis's own retroductive finding — that status inversion functions as psychological compensation for structural domination unavailable through ordinary political channels — is, if anything, reinforced by this period's evidence, since the same mechanism recurs after both the movement's move from a marginal 1930s sect to a large mid-century institution and, later, that institution's own succession crisis. The newly incorporated 1960–1963 Malcolm X material shows, further, that both frames survived and even flourished under repeated public re-elaboration across markedly different registers (mass civic rally, elite law-school forum, broadcast interview, university lecture hall) well before any hint of their eventual contestation, which strengthens rather than weakens the case that their later abandonment by Malcolm X was a substantive, considered break rather than a difference of degree or a matter of insufficient exposure to the doctrine. M2 (Loyalty and Hypocrisy) has a structural precursor in the Period 1 corpus's account of "Rebels against the Will of Allah" and factional schism (Beynon, 1938), but the 1952–1977 corpus documents this frame's *lethal-register* deployment with a specificity ("worthy of death") the Period 1 material does not contain, and this expanded edition further shows that Malcolm X himself had long deployed a structurally identical loyalty/hypocrisy binary against other Black leaders before it was turned against him. M3, M5, and M6 are this period's genuinely new motivational developments, generated respectively by direct international contact with the non-racialized Muslim world, by engagement with a bounded electoral political calendar unavailable to the much smaller 1930s movement, and by a documented, individually embodied experience of political violence — all three keyed to historical circumstances (decolonization, the 1964 election, the escalating violence of the civil rights and post-schism era) that postdate, and were unavailable to, the Period 1 founders.
