# Chapter Outline
## Divergent Frame Projects After Elijah Muhammad, 1978–1995

### Evidence rule

The chapter must be written from the corpus, not from general summaries. The present period-3 pilot contains eight readable text files: five Farrakhan sources (1978, 1980 x3, 1982) and three Warith Deen Mohammed sources (1979, 1980, 1995). The archive currently exposes no fifty-source text sample for either leader in this period. One period-labeled PDF remains to be OCR-processed. Every quotation should identify leader, date, title, archive filename, and page or scan location where available.

## 1. Introduction: the succession crisis as a framing contest

- Research question: how did Farrakhan and Warith Deen Mohammed represent the post-Elijah problem, propose futures, and motivate action?
- Thesis: both inherited language of Black historical injury, knowledge, discipline, and divine responsibility, but configured it into different leadership projects.
- Evidence boundaries: eight direct text files; no claims of frequency or reception beyond the sample.
- Distinguish frame from outcome: rhetoric establishes represented problems and requested action, not compliance.

## 2. Method and corpus design

- Diagnostic, prognostic, and motivational framing.
- Perspectival Critical Realism: empirical text, advocated practice, documented practice, and possible generative mechanisms.
- Source register fields: leader, date, title, genre, publication venue, audience, filename, PDF/OCR status, quotation, page/scan reference.
- Sampling rule: add and read up to fifty sources per leader where available; report the actual count if fewer exist.
- Citation protocol: filename plus page or stable text location; flag OCR uncertainty.

## 3. Inheritance, authority, and the problem of continuity

- Elijah Muhammad as teacher, guide, programme, memory, and contested authority.
- Farrakhan: Elijah Muhammad as “divine leader,” “divine teacher,” and “divine guide”; restoration through the Twelve Point Programme.
- Mohammed: leadership as community representation, discernment, and support for worthy leaders.
- Succession as different objects: restoration of a divinely authorised national programme versus distributed, recognisable leadership.

## 4. Farrakhan’s diagnostic frame

- Lost from land, people, self, and “the power of our own being” (1978).
- White supremacy and “black ignorance” as material and epistemic domination.
- Branch knowledge, religious/ideological fragmentation, and the need for root knowledge.
- Internal moral disorder: smoking, drugs, sexual betrayal, greed, status-seeking, weak trust and family life (1980 program speech).
- Post-Elijah contamination: hypocrisy, deceit, and the serpent metaphor (1982).

## 5. Farrakhan’s prognostic frame

- Mastery of self as the precondition for mastery of the social world.
- Service replacing power-seeking.
- Homes, schools, hospitals, factories, land, and knowledge.
- Separation as protection and as the institutional form of submission to Allah.
- Reinstating Elijah Muhammad’s programme as practical reconstruction rather than nostalgic memory.

## 6. Farrakhan’s motivational frame

- Divine appointment and inherited obligation.
- The 1934–1974 “first term” and post-Elijah examination.
- Direct address, rebuke, urgency, sacrifice, and moral purification.
- Prophetic models of confrontation.
- Hope through dignity, family, service, spiritual elevation, and Black institutional power.

## 7. Mohammed’s diagnostic frame

- Leadership failure, jealousy, materialism, lust, corruption, and human weakness (1979).
- Institutional drift from liberation into aristocracy, dictatorship, materialism, and elite scholarship (1980).
- Failure to educate and liberate ordinary people.
- Bilal as a sign exposing the gap between Islamic universalism and actual practice.
- Racial domination as deprivation of names, culture, religion, knowledge, and political representation.

## 8. Mohammed’s prognostic frame

- Strong but accountable leadership.
- Community participation in forming and correcting leadership.
- Universal humanitarian liberation and education for all people.
- Public proclamation: “take it to the top of the house” and let the light shine.
- Sincere prayer as an antidote to corruption.
- Distributed succession through several capable leaders rather than one named successor (1995).

## 9. Mohammed’s motivational frame

- Confidence in communal judgment.
- Mature support for worthy leadership without dictatorship.
- Bilal as a motivating identity and prophetic sign.
- Universal audience construction.
- Calm continuity and attention to living community practice rather than death or replacement.

## 10. Comparative frame configurations

### Configuration A: Farrakhan

Historical dispossession + white supremacy + epistemic loss + internal disorder → fragmented and vulnerable Black collective → self-mastery, root knowledge, service, independent institutions, and restored Elijah Muhammad programme → action as divine duty and survival.

### Configuration B: Mohammed

Leadership failure + materialism/corruption + institutional stagnation + racial exclusion → community unable to fulfil liberation and education → accountable leadership, sincere Islam, universal humanitarian mission, public teaching, and distributed succession → action as mature religious and civic responsibility.

### Comparative questions

- What does “knowledge” name in each corpus?
- Is unity organised through a distinct Black nation, a universal Islamic mission, or both?
- How does each leader define legitimate continuity with Elijah Muhammad?
- What forms of discipline are requested, and who is authorised to enforce them?
- How do domestic, educational, economic, civic, and religious futures relate?

## 11. Temporal development

- 1978–1980: early consolidation and explicit programme formation.
- 1981–1989: add up to fifty-source samples before making claims about differentiation or stabilisation.
- 1990–1995: compare retrospective succession language and later institutional self-understanding.
- Separate genuine temporal change from changes in genre, venue, editorial selection, and audience.

## 12. Retroduction and mechanisms

- Identity disruption after succession.
- Institutional vulnerability and organisational rebuilding.
- Epistemic marginalisation and recovery of historical knowledge.
- Religious interpretation as political and moral capacity.
- Authority contestation and different models of succession.
- Alternative explanations: genre, personal style, immediate audience, editorial mediation, and strategic coalition-building.

## 13. Conclusion

- Answer what each leader represented as the problem.
- Identify the future each made thinkable and the subject each attempted to produce.
- State what can and cannot be inferred from the texts.
- Reserve claims about mobilisation, reception, and institutional effects for evidence beyond rhetoric.
